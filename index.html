PROBLEM 03  ·  REVENUE MECHANICS
Solved by TrooGrowth →

Bundling, pricing and subscription mechanics are underused

Wellbeing has said publicly that nearly half its customers come back within 12 months, and that about 60% of website revenue comes from repeat customers. Three revenue levers are either missing or working against each other.

AOV · BUNDLES

Bundles and add-ons are missing where they would lift order value

Nine in ten products in stock cost ₹2,000 or less, with a median of ₹649, yet the cart's free gifts start at ₹4,499 and ₹7,999. Seven of the eleven combo, kit and gift-box listings in the store are unavailable, including the Superhero Melts Gift Box, three weeks before Dhanteras. Ready-made bundles such as a Sleep Kit (Triple Magnesium with Sleep Melts), a Glow Kit (collagen with glutathione) and a pre-Diwali gift bundle, plus add-on suggestions in the cart drawer matched to what is already in it, would close the gap between a typical basket and the gift thresholds.

PRICING · OFFERS

Discounts are stacked but not structured

On Triple Magnesium the banner promises "up to 25%", the product is 15% off, the member price is 20% off, and the Pack of 2 saves the same 15% as a single pack. Omega ads promise "15% OFF" while the Omega page shows 25%. The experiments worth running: a real step-up saving on multi-packs, price per serving shown on every pack, a prepaid incentive shown before checkout, and one genuine pre-Diwali offer with a real end date in place of the resetting cart timer.

RETENTION

No subscribe and save on a catalogue built for daily use

None of the nine hero products offers a subscription. Wellbeing has said nearly half its customers return within 12 months and about 60% of website revenue comes from repeat buyers. Marine Collagen asks for 12 weeks of daily use from a 25-serving tub, yet the packs show only a percentage saving. Setu offers an extra 10% on subscription and Moon Juice 30%.

Where the page loses them
Conversion barriers, by priority
HIGH PRIORITY

Clinical claims are contradicted by the page's own disclaimer

WHAT THE SHOPPER WANTS HERE

"I'm paying a premium because it says it works. Show me it works."

The page carries a "Clinically Proven" badge and footnoted benefits such as "Prevents Migraines & Headaches". Directly under the description it says: "Claims are ingredient-based and not for the product." For a careful buyer that line reads as the brand withdrawing every claim above it. Nothing on the page connects the cited studies to the 220 mg in this capsule, so the evidence section persuades nobody who actually reads it. The FAQ also answers "Yes" to whether the capsule helps with PCOS, on the same page that says its claims are not for the product, which is exactly the shopper managing cramps, PMS or PCOS who needs a straight answer.

RECOMMENDATION

Replace "Clinically Proven" with what is true, for example "Forms backed by clinical research". For each benefit, show ingredient, study, dose used in the study and dose in this capsule, side by side.

HIGH PRIORITY

The elemental dose is not stated in the page text

WHAT THE SHOPPER WANTS HERE

"Tell me the dose per serving, in words, before I buy."

The page text never states the dose. It appears only on the back-of-pack photo, ninth in the gallery: 220 mg elemental magnesium, 50% of the RDA, from a 1,000 mg blend of three forms. The split between bisglycinate, threonate and Aquamin citrate is not stated anywhere. Setu leads with 300 mg elemental and "No Proprietary Blends"; BiOptimizers states 500 mg elemental and compares itself with single-form magnesiums. Against them, Wellbeing looks like it is hiding the one number this buyer checks first.

RECOMMENDATION

A supplement-facts panel in text directly under the price: 220 mg elemental, the mg of each form, and one line on what each form is for.

HIGH PRIORITY

The cited study tests a form the page advises against

WHAT THE SHOPPER WANTS HERE

"If the science is real, the footnotes should hold up."

The cramps claim is footnoted to a 2017 JAMA Internal Medicine study on magnesium oxide. The page's own FAQ tells shoppers to choose glycinate, malate or taurate "rather than cheaper oxide variants", and the product contains no oxide. One click on a footnote undoes the credibility of the entire evidence block, for exactly the buyer who clicks footnotes.

RECOMMENDATION

Replace the study with one on the forms actually used, or drop the cramps claim until there is one.

HIGH PRIORITY

Three conflicting results timelines

WHAT THE SHOPPER WANTS HERE

"Give me one honest answer so I know when to judge it."

A review says "from the 1st day", the FAQ says 1 to 2 weeks, and a gallery image says visible results start at 4 to 6 weeks. A shopper who expects night one and feels nothing by night five has every reason not to reorder. Setu gives one line: meaningful changes within 1 to 2 weeks of nightly use.

RECOMMENDATION

One reconciled timeline on the page, for example: calmer in the first nights, better sleep by week two, cramps and focus at four to six weeks.

See the remaining barriers
MEDIUM PRIORITY

No risk reversal near the purchase button

WHAT THE SHOPPER WANTS HERE

"Let me try it without risking ₹1,189 on something that might not work for me."

Nothing near the button addresses this. The refunds page accepts returns only for products damaged in transit, defective or shipped wrong. Setu offers a 60-day money-back guarantee, BiOptimizers 365 days and Moon Juice 45 days, all stated on the product page.

RECOMMENDATION

Decide whether a sleep guarantee is viable. If it is not, state the actual policy plainly next to the button so it is not discovered after purchase.

MEDIUM PRIORITY

No safety guidance for nightly use

WHAT THE SHOPPER WANTS HERE

"I need to know this is safe to take long term, and safe for me."

The words habit-forming, pregnancy and kidney do not appear anywhere on the page, for a capsule taken nightly before bed. Setu and Moon Juice both state non-habit-forming; BiOptimizers tells shoppers with kidney concerns or on blood pressure medication to check with a practitioner. Wellbeing's own Sleep Melts ads say "non-habit-forming"; this page never does, for the sleep-struggler most likely to worry about depending on it.

RECOMMENDATION

A short safety block in the buy box: non-habit-forming (if true), who should check with a doctor first, and the medication note already in the FAQ.

MEDIUM PRIORITY

The Pack of 2 offers no additional saving

WHAT THE SHOPPER WANTS HERE

"If I'm committing to two months, reward me for it."

Pack of 1 and Pack of 2 both save 15%, while the banner across the top of the page advertises "up to 25%" and the member price is 20% off. At ₹1,189 for 30 servings this is about ₹40 a night, and the page never says so. Setu shows ₹46 per serve and an extra 10% on subscription.

RECOMMENDATION

Give the Pack of 2 a real extra saving, show the per-night price, and add subscribe and save on a 30-night cycle.

MEDIUM PRIORITY

The stock counter displays a random figure

WHAT THE SHOPPER WANTS HERE

"If you're rushing me, I want the reason to be real."

Two fresh visits 21 seconds apart showed "Only 31 left in stock" and "Only 22 left in stock". The page as sent by the server says 7,604. None of the five competitors we checked shows a stock counter at all.

RECOMMENDATION

Remove the counter. If urgency is needed, use a true one, such as "30-night supply, reorder by night 25".

LOWER PRIORITY

Reviews lack balance and outcome detail

WHAT THE SHOPPER WANTS HERE

"Show me the honest reviews, not just the glowing ones."

The breakdown is 709 five-star, 71 four-star, 2 two-star and none at one or three stars, and the first reviews shown include "Good product" and "Loved it". Swisse shows its 3-star and 2-star reviews openly in a 191-review breakdown.

RECOMMENDATION

Tag reviews by outcome (sleep, cramps, migraines), surface a helpful critical review with a brand reply, and lead with reviews that describe results.

LOWER PRIORITY

No guidance on choosing between magnesium products

WHAT THE SHOPPER WANTS HERE

"Help me pick the right one, even if it's not this one."

The page points to Ultra Strength and Magnesium Glycinate in one line of copy, with no comparison of who each is for. A shopper buying for cramps and recovery cannot tell whether this capsule or Ultra Strength is meant for her.

RECOMMENDATION

A three-way chooser by goal: sleep, recovery, all-round.

LOWER PRIORITY

No delivery information on the page

WHAT THE SHOPPER WANTS HERE

"Tell me before checkout, not during it."

There is no delivery estimate, pincode check or shipping line on the page. GNC shows delivery options with a pincode check and a COD note on its product page.

RECOMMENDATION

Pincode ETA and shipping terms directly under the button.
